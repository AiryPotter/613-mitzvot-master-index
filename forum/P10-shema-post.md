# Mitzvah 10: The Shema — What It Is and How It Is Said

The Shema is often called the central declaration of Jewish faith, and reciting it is Mitzvah 10 in our guide (Rambam, Positive Commandment 10). Its source is Deuteronomy 6:7: *"thou shalt talk of them… when thou liest down, and when thou risest up."* The tradition reads this as a command to say these words every evening and every morning, at the times when people lie down and get up. Maimonides counts it as a commandment from the Torah itself (citing Talmud Berakhot 21a). Because it needs no Temple, land, or court, it has been observed without interruption to this day.

**What is said.** The Shema is made up of three Torah passages: Deuteronomy 6:4–9 ("Hear, O Israel… the LORD is one"), Deuteronomy 11:13–21, and Numbers 15:37–41. Maimonides explains the order: the first passage declares God's unity and the love of God, the second speaks of accepting the commandments, and the third gives the commandment of tzitzit and recalls the Exodus. After the first verse, the line *Barukh shem kevod malkhuto le-olam va-ed* ("Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom forever and ever") is said quietly.

**How it is traditionally performed.** In the evening, the preferred time is from nightfall to midnight; in the morning, ideally finishing by sunrise, and no later than the end of the third hour of the day. Rabbinic blessings frame it: two before and one after in the morning, and two before and two after in the evening. The essential requirement is intention: according to Maimonides, one who does not concentrate on the meaning of the first verse has not fulfilled the commandment. Many people cover their eyes with the right hand while saying that verse, a practice the Talmud traces to Rabbi Judah the Prince. It may be said sitting, standing, or walking (pausing for the first verse), loudly enough to hear oneself, and in any language one understands. Many also recite it at bedtime.

**Who is obligated.** Classically, adult men. The Mishnah exempts women because the commandment has a fixed time, though many women recite it today.

**For discussion:** The Talmud debates how much of the Shema the Torah itself requires: all three passages, the first section, or just the first verse. Why might the rabbis have joined these three passages together? And what do you make of Maimonides' view that the first verse alone requires full concentration?
